    About This Guide

    AI is now part of almost every tool you use for school. AI drafts, summarizes, and rewrites in seconds. Speed makes AI useful, and speed also makes it easy to skip the work your courses are built around. The Student AI Guide explains what you learn by doing your own coursework, where AI falls short, and how to use AI within your course’s rules. The guide is based on Chapter Twelve of the Code of Student Conduct and the university’s policy on AI in academic work.

    Your instructor may ask you to complete the guide and upload a certificate to the course. Every course uses these three steps:

    1. Read the guide. Ten short sections cover doing the work yourself, the limits of AI, AI levels, tools with AI built in, disclosure, and where to get help.
    2. Take the quiz. The quiz shows eight questions one at a time. A wrong answer explains why and links back to the section. You can try again as often as you need.
    3. Download your certificate. Enter your name and course number. Make one certificate for each course that asks for it.

    Your syllabus and your assignment instructions set the AI rules for each course. Where this guide and the Code of Student Conduct differ, the Code governs. Your quiz answers, your name, your course number, and your certificate stay on your own device, and the website stores none of them. The site counts page visits and certificate downloads.

      Section 1 of 10

      1Doing the Work Yourself

      AI is now built into the tools you use every day, from your word processor to your search engine. AI makes producing work easy: a paragraph, a summary, or an outline appears in seconds. AI also makes it easy to hand over your thinking. You accept an answer because the answer sounds right, and you stop weighing AI output against your own judgment. Researchers call accepting AI answers with little scrutiny cognitive surrender (Shaw & Nave, 2026).

      Your degree is a certification of what you can do. You learn those skills by doing the work during your education (and the work is hard). For example, the effort of drafting an argument, working through a hard reading, or revising a weak paragraph is where the learning happens. A task you hand to AI before you can do it yourself is a skill you skip building. Experienced professionals hand AI routine work they have done many times and can check. They keep the decisions that require their judgment for themselves. As a student, you are building that experience now. Teachers, counselors, school leaders, and other education professionals make decisions that depend on their own judgment. Your coursework is where you practice making those decisions.

      2Limits of AI

      AI learned patterns from human writing, which is why its text sounds right. Checking the accuracy of AI text is your job. AI is unreliable for current facts, citations, data interpretation, and answers to assignment questions. Submitting AI output unchanged is the clearest case of misuse.

      3The AI Level in Your Syllabus

      Your instructor sets the AI level for each course and states the level in the syllabus. Your syllabus may use a statement from Georgia Southern’s AI Statement Builder, which describes the three levels below. The syllabus level is the default, and an assignment’s instructions can change the level: an Open Use course may have an exam at the No AI Use level, and a No AI Use course may have one assignment where using AI is the task. When the syllabus and the assignment instructions differ, follow the assignment instructions.

      Read the AI statement in each syllabus and the instructions for each assignment before you start. Ask your instructor before you use AI whenever you are unsure, including when the syllabus and the assignment instructions are silent on AI. A tool your instructor allowed last semester may be off limits in this course.

      LevelWhat it allows
      No AI UseYour own work only. Spelling and grammar checkers built into Word or Google Docs are fine.
      Restricted UseAI for the uses your syllabus lists, such as brainstorming or feedback on a draft. Everything else is your own work. You disclose any AI you used.
      Open UseBroader AI use, which you disclose. Your own analysis and choices must be visible in the work.

      Two uses of AI are violations at every level: AI that does the work for you, and AI use you keep hidden. Under the Code of Student Conduct, using AI “to perform central requirements of an assignment” without permission is cheating (Ch. 12, A.1.a). Presenting AI-generated text as your own is plagiarism (Ch. 12, A.4.a).

      4Tools with AI Built In

      Some AI works like the spell checker you already know (e.g., Grammarly). It flags a misspelled word or a missing comma for you to fix. Generative AI writes for you: it rewrites your sentences, drafts paragraphs, summarizes readings, and produces outlines from a prompt. Many familiar tools, including Grammarly, now do both.

      ToolAcceptable at every levelCounts as AI use
      GrammarlySpelling and punctuation flagsRewrites of sentences or paragraphs, tone changes, text generated from a prompt
      Microsoft Word, Google DocsBuilt-in spelling and grammar checkCopilot or Gemini drafting, rewriting, or summarizing
      QuillBot and other paraphrasersNoneAny paraphrasing of your text or a source
      AI reading summaries (PDF readers, browser add-ons)NoneSummaries you read in place of the source

      With Grammarly, it is easy to use generative AI by accident. Its rewrite suggestions share one panel with its spelling fixes. One click replaces your sentence with Grammarly’s version. In a No AI Use course, turn Grammarly off and use the checker in Word or Google Docs.

      5Common Situations

      “I wrote it myself, then Grammarly rewrote some paragraphs.”

      In a No AI Use course, the rewritten paragraphs are AI-generated text. In a Restricted or Open Use course, check whether the syllabus allows AI editing. If it does, disclose which paragraphs Grammarly rewrote.

      “I asked ChatGPT to find sources.”

      AI tools invent citations that look real. A fabricated citation is cheating at every level, including Open Use (Ch. 12, A.1.c). Find sources through the library or Google Scholar. Open and read every source you cite.

      “AI summarized the reading, and I wrote from the summary.”

      Reading and synthesizing are central requirements in most courses. Read the source unless your syllabus allows AI summaries.

      6Disclosing AI Use

      Disclose every use of AI unless your syllabus says otherwise. Put a short statement at the end of the assignment:

      I used [tool] to [task, e.g., suggest a structure for section 2 / check the clarity of my conclusion]. I reviewed and revised its suggestions. The analysis and final wording are my own.

      Your instructor may ask for more, such as the prompts you used.

      7Your Drafts as Your Record

      Keep your drafts, notes, and version history for every assignment. The Office of Student Conduct looks at your writing process when an instructor has questions about a submission. It checks version history in Word or Google Docs, document properties, Grammarly’s revision history, and your ability to explain and discuss the work. A student with a draft history can show how the work developed. Writing in Word or Google Docs with version history turned on gives you that history.

      8Instructor Questions About Your Work

      An instructor who suspects unauthorized AI use files a report with the Office of the Provost. The instructor then asks to meet with you. You receive written notice at least three class days before the meeting. You can see and respond to all the evidence the instructor relies on. You can also bring a witness (Ch. 12, C.1.a). A detection tool result is a reason for your instructor to talk with you. Your instructor decides the case after talking with you. A first-time case is usually settled with the instructor. If you disagree with the instructor’s resolution, you can ask for a formal hearing.

      In a first-time case, the instructor decides the sanction for a finding of responsibility. The sanction can range from an educational sanction, such as a research paper, to a lower grade on the assignment, up to a complete loss of credit for the course. Students who fabricate sources or data face the most serious outcomes. While a case is open, the assignment grade stays as it is. Your course grade is recorded as Incomplete if final grades are due while the case is open. The Office of the Provost keeps a record of every finding. A student with a prior finding goes directly to the Office of Student Conduct. The Office of Student Conduct can add a disciplinary warning, probation, suspension, or expulsion.

      9Support on Campus

      When an assignment feels hard, seek help from a person before you turn to AI. Seeking help is part of doing the work yourself. Seeking it early can also prevent an academic integrity case.

      The Writing Center

      Tutors work with you at any stage of a paper: planning it, organizing a draft, strengthening an argument, or revising after feedback. They help you improve your own writing, and you do the proofreading and editing yourself. Meet in person at Henderson Library (Statesboro) or Lane Library (Armstrong), or by video. Schedule at georgiasouthern.mywconline.com.

      Your Instructor’s Office Hours

      Office hours are for every student, at every stage of an assignment. Bring your questions about the expectations and the rubric before you start. Bring an outline or a rough draft when you are stuck or unsure how to organize your ideas. Bring a graded paper to go over the feedback and how to use it on the next assignment. Your instructor expects you to grow as a writer and holds office hours to support your growth.
